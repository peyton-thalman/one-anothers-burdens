# One Another's Burdens
### A gospel companion for those who love someone who is suffering

*Adapted from the Church's* Support Guide: Help for Spouses and Family of Those in Recovery *and widened to cover any affliction. An independent study guide, not an official publication of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.*

---

## Start here: what this is and how to use it

The Church's *Support Guide* was written for spouses and family members of people working through addiction. Its twelve principles turn out to fit far more than addiction. Almost every one of them is built on a scripture about affliction in general: God consoling us in our afflictions, the Savior taking on our pains and sicknesses, friends standing by us, bearing hard things with patience.

This guide keeps those twelve principles and their scriptures, and rewrites everything around them for anyone who loves someone who is suffering. That could be a spouse with cancer, a child with a disability, a parent with dementia, a son or daughter with depression or anxiety, a loved one caught in addiction, a family member who has stepped away from the Church, a marriage under strain, or the long ache of grief after a death. If you are the one carrying the affliction, most of it applies to you too.

It's built for three uses:

1. **Personal study.** One chapter a week, with a Practice Card to try before the next one.
2. **Couples and families.** Read a chapter together and talk through the questions.
3. **Groups.** A ward class, a Relief Society or elders quorum discussion, or a support group. Each chapter fits a 45 to 60 minute meeting.

### Who this is for

You don't have to be in crisis to use this. Some afflictions arrive overnight with a diagnosis or a phone call. Others build slowly for years. Some will be healed in this life and some won't. This guide tries to be honest about all of those.

A few of the situations it has in mind:

- **Physical illness and injury**, short or chronic, treatable or terminal
- **Disability**, from birth or acquired later
- **Mental and emotional illness**, including depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, eating disorders, and thoughts of suicide
- **Addiction and compulsive behavior**, the subject of the original guide
- **Aging and dementia** in a parent or spouse
- **Grief**, after a death, a miscarriage, a divorce, or a loss that no one else can see
- **A loved one's choices**, such as leaving the Church, breaking covenants, or cutting off the family

### The main goal: make it stick

A good lesson can feel wonderful on Sunday and change nothing by Wednesday. Alma taught that "by small and simple things are great things brought to pass" (Alma 37:6). That is also what research on habits keeps finding. People change through small, specific practices, repeated, with someone checking in.

A few principles behind every Practice Card:

- **One thing per week.** Pick the single practice most likely to help. Doing one thing builds more faith than half doing five.
- **Make it an if-then plan.** "I'll pray more" rarely happens. "When I sit in the car before going into the hospital, I'll say a short prayer" usually does. Psychologists call these *implementation intentions*. Across nearly a hundred studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006), people who planned this way were much more likely to follow through.
- **Shrink it until it's easy.** Caregivers are tired. Make the starting version so small it feels almost silly: one verse, one deep breath, one text to a friend. You can always do more.
- **Plan for missing a day.** You will. One missed day is life. Two in a row is the start of a new pattern, so just pick it back up.
- **Tell someone.** If you're in a group, report back each week. If you're on your own, tell a spouse, friend, or ministering sister or brother what you're trying.

### A note on safety

This is a study guide. It is not therapy and it is not medical advice. Some situations need professional help, and seeking it is an act of faith, not a lack of it. Keep these numbers close:

- **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** (US): call or text **988**
- **Crisis Text Line**: text **HOME** to **741741**
- **National Domestic Violence Hotline**: **1-800-799-7233**, or text **START** to **88788**
- **SAMHSA National Helpline** (substance use, free, 24/7): **1-800-662-4357**
- **Emergency**: **911**

Your bishop can also help you get counseling through Family Services.

## Chapter 1: God Will Console Us in Our Afflictions
*Manual themes: God's help in any situation, He knows what we face, He will not abandon us, Comfort during the trial*

> "Look unto God with firmness of mind, and pray unto him with exceeding faith, and he will console you in your afflictions." (Jacob 3:1)

### The Big Idea
When someone you love is suffering, it can feel like the ground has moved. You may be scared, worn out, angry, or numb, sometimes all in the same afternoon. The first principle is the simplest one. God knows exactly what you and your loved one are facing, He has not stepped away, and He will comfort you in it. Comfort is not the same as removing the trial. Often the trial stays for a while and the comfort comes inside it.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **Jacob 3:1.** Jacob was speaking to people who had been badly hurt by others. His promise is that God "will console you in your afflictions." The word is *in*. The consolation comes during the hard thing.
- **Isaiah 49:15–16.** "Can a woman forget her sucking child? … yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands." God compares His attention to a nursing mother's, then says His is even more constant.
- **Hebrews 13:5.** "I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee."
- **John 11:35.** "Jesus wept." He wept at Lazarus's tomb minutes before raising him. He knew how the story would end and still grieved with the people He loved.
- **Alma 36:3.** "Whosoever shall put their trust in God shall be supported in their trials, and their troubles, and their afflictions."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "He does love you, and He knows your fears. He hears your prayers. He is your Heavenly Father, and surely He matches with His own the tears His children shed." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["An High Priest of Good Things to Come"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1999/10/an-high-priest-of-good-things-to-come?lang=eng) (October 1999)

> "If you are lonely, please know you can find comfort. If you are discouraged, please know you can find hope. If you are poor in spirit, please know you can be strengthened. If you feel you are broken, please know you can be mended." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Broken Things to Mend"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/04/broken-things-to-mend?lang=eng) (April 2006)

> "When a spouse dies, a companion will wonder. When financial hardship befalls a family, a father will ask. When children wander from the path, a mother and father will cry out in sorrow. Yes, 'weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.'" — Elder Robert D. Hales, ["Waiting upon the Lord: Thy Will Be Done"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2011/10/waiting-upon-the-lord-thy-will-be-done?lang=eng) (October 2011)

### In Real Life
- **A new diagnosis.** The first weeks are often a blur of appointments and information. It's normal to have a hard time praying. Short, honest prayers count: "Please help me get through today."
- **A long illness.** Comfort in a chronic illness tends to come in small, repeated ways: a good day, a kind nurse, a verse that lands. Writing them down helps you see them.
- **A loved one's choices.** When the affliction comes from someone's choices, it can feel like God should step in and stop them. He usually won't override their agency, but He will stand with you.
- **Grief.** Comfort and grief can sit side by side. Feeling comforted doesn't mean you're done grieving.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Prayer and coping.** Researchers who study religious coping (Kenneth Pargament and others) consistently find that people who feel supported by God in a crisis tend to cope better than those who feel abandoned or punished by Him. How you picture God in the hard moment matters.
- **Shock is normal.** Early reactions to bad news, like numbness, forgetting things, or trouble sleeping, are common stress responses, not signs of weak faith. They usually ease with time and support.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. When have you felt comforted during a trial in the past, even if the trial didn't end? What did that comfort look like?
2. What do you most want God to know about what you're carrying right now? Have you told Him in plain words?
3. Is there a way you picture God that makes it harder to turn to Him right now? Where did that picture come from?
4. Who in the scriptures do you relate to most in this season, and why?

### Practice Card: Week 1
**Main practice: The comfort list**
Each night this week, write down one way you felt helped, comforted, or noticed that day, however small.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (turn off the light / plug in my phone / finish dinner), I will write one line in my comfort list.

**Also try:**
- **Pray plainly.** Once this week, pray out loud for five minutes and say exactly how you feel, without dressing it up.
- **Carry a verse.** Write Jacob 3:1 or Isaiah 49:16 on a card and keep it in your pocket, car, or phone case.

Holland, ["An High Priest of Good Things to Come"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1999/10/an-high-priest-of-good-things-to-come?lang=eng) · Robert D. ## Chapter 2: Shake Off the Chains with Which Ye Are Bound
*Manual themes: Letting go of blame, We are children of God, We are still free to act*

> "Awake … put on the armor of righteousness. Shake off the chains with which ye are bound, and come forth out of obscurity, and arise from the dust." (2 Nephi 1:23)

### The Big Idea
Families of people who suffer often carry chains no one else can see: guilt, blame, shame, and the feeling that their own life is on hold. Maybe you think you should have noticed sooner, or that something you did caused this. Maybe you've stopped doing things you love because it feels wrong to enjoy anything while someone you love is hurting. This principle asks you to set those chains down. Your loved one's affliction is not a verdict on you. You are still a child of God with your own life, your own growth, and your own agency.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **John 9:2–3.** The disciples asked, "Master, who did sin, this man, or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus answered, "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him." The urge to find someone to blame is very old, and the Savior rejected it.
- **Romans 8:16–17.** "The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God: And if children, then heirs." Your worth doesn't rise or fall with your family's circumstances.
- **2 Nephi 2:27.** We are "free to choose liberty and eternal life." Even when you didn't choose the affliction, you still choose how you respond to it.
- **2 Nephi 1:23.** Lehi spoke these words near the end of his life to sons he worried about. He told them to arise from the dust, not to stay there.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "His parents do not ask, 'Why did this happen to our noble son, who was serving at the call of the Master?' No one has a certain answer except perhaps in circumstances where higher purposes are served. We must walk in faith." — Elder James E. Faust, ["Hope, an Anchor of the Soul"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1999/10/hope-an-anchor-of-the-soul?lang=eng) (October 1999), speaking of a missionary paralyzed in an accident

> "He said, 'I don't think the Lord would want you to punish yourself for the death of your little boy.' I felt the love of my Heavenly Father through one of his chosen vessels." — Elder Shayne M. Bowen, ["'Because I Live, Ye Shall Live Also'"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2012/10/because-i-live-ye-shall-live-also?lang=eng) (October 2012), remembering counsel he received after his son died

> "These afflictions are some of the realities of mortal life, and there should be no more shame in acknowledging them than in acknowledging a battle with high blood pressure or the sudden appearance of a malignant tumor." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) (October 2013)

### In Real Life
- **Parents of a child with a disability or illness** often search their own history for a cause: something they ate, a decision they made. Most of the time there was nothing to find. Even when there was, guilt doesn't help your child. Love and steady care do.
- **Mental illness** is still surrounded by shame in many families. Elder Holland's point is that it deserves no more shame than heart disease. Talking about it openly is part of shaking off the chain.
- **When the affliction involves a loved one's choices,** such as addiction, infidelity, or leaving the Church, you are not responsible for those choices. You may have made mistakes. Everyone has. But their agency is theirs.
- **Your own life counts.** It's not selfish to keep going to work, see friends, laugh, or serve in a calling. Your loved one usually wants that for you, even if they can't say it.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Self-blame makes caregiving harder.** Studies of family caregivers find that guilt and self-blame are linked with more depression and burnout. Self-compassion, treating yourself the way you'd treat a friend in the same spot (Kristin Neff's research), is linked with better coping and more patience with the person you're caring for.
- **"Ambiguous loss."** Family therapist Pauline Boss coined this term for losses without clear edges: a parent with dementia who is here but not here, or a child who is alive but estranged. These losses are hard to grieve because no one marks them. Naming it helps.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. What do you blame yourself for in your loved one's situation? Would you blame a friend in the same position?
2. What have you stopped doing since this started? Which of those things would be healthy to pick back up?
3. If you fully believed you are a child of God whose worth doesn't depend on how things turn out, what would change in how you spend this week?
4. What is one choice that is still fully yours, even in this situation?

### Practice Card: Week 2
**Main practice: Set one chain down**
Write down one thing you've been blaming yourself for. Underneath, write what you'd say to a friend who told you the same thing. Read it each morning this week.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (pour my first drink of the day / sit down in the car), I will read what I wrote.

**Also try:**
- **One thing for you.** Do one small thing this week that you used to enjoy and stopped doing.
- **Say it out loud.** Tell one trusted person what you've been carrying. Shame shrinks when it's spoken.

### Go Deeper
- **Talks:** Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) · Shayne M. Bowen, ["'Because I Live, Ye Shall Live Also'"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2012/10/because-i-live-ye-shall-live-also?lang=eng)
- **Scriptures:** John 9:1–7 · 2 Nephi 2:25–27 · Moses 1:39
- **Books:** *Ambiguous Loss*, Pauline Boss · *Self-Compassion*, Kristin Neff · *Weakness Is Not Sin*, Wendy Ulrich

## Chapter 3: He Will Take upon Him the Pains and the Sicknesses of His People
*Manual themes: No blessing will be denied, Christ can heal our deepest pain, Giving our burdens to the Lord, Healing takes time*

> "He will take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people. … And he will take upon him their infirmities, that his bowels may be filled with mercy, according to the flesh, that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people according to their infirmities." (Alma 7:11–12)

### The Big Idea
Most of us think of the Atonement of Jesus Christ as being about sin. Alma 7 says it is also about pain, sickness, and infirmity. The Savior chose to experience all of it so He would know, from the inside, how to help. That includes the pain of the person you love and your own pain watching them. You don't have to explain your situation to Him. He already knows what it feels like.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **Alma 7:11–12.** He took on our pains "that he may know … how to succor his people." To *succor* means to run to someone's aid.
- **Isaiah 53:4.** "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows."
- **Mosiah 24:14–15.** The Lord promised Alma's people He would ease their burdens. He didn't remove them at first. He strengthened the people "that they could bear up their burdens with ease."
- **Matthew 11:28–30.** "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. … For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light." A yoke is shared. The invitation is to pull alongside Him.
- **Isaiah 61:3.** He came "to give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "Thus, the Savior has suffered not just for our sins and iniquities—but also for our physical pains and anguish, our weaknesses and shortcomings, our fears and frustrations, our disappointments and discouragement, our regrets and remorse, our despair and desperation, the injustices and inequities we experience, and the emotional distresses that beset us." — Elder David A. Bednar, ["Bear Up Their Burdens with Ease"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2014/04/bear-up-their-burdens-with-ease?lang=eng) (April 2014)

> "He therefore knows our struggles, our heartaches, our temptations, and our suffering, for He willingly experienced them all as an essential part of His Atonement. And because of this, His Atonement empowers Him to succor us—to give us the strength to bear it all." — Elder Dallin H. Oaks, ["Strengthened by the Atonement of Jesus Christ"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2015/10/strengthened-by-the-atonement-of-jesus-christ?lang=eng) (October 2015)

> "I testify that the Savior's Atonement lifts from us not only the burden of our sins but also the burden of our disappointments and sorrows, our heartaches and our despair." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Broken Things to Mend"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/04/broken-things-to-mend?lang=eng) (April 2006)

> "In the eternal scheme of things, wrongs will be righted. In the perfect justice of the Lord, all who live worthily will be compensated for blessings not enjoyed here." — Elder James E. Faust, ["Hope, an Anchor of the Soul"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1999/10/hope-an-anchor-of-the-soul?lang=eng) (October 1999)

### In Real Life
- **When healing comes.** Sometimes treatment works, a child recovers, a loved one comes home. Thank God for it openly.
- **When healing is slow or doesn't come in this life.** Many afflictions, like a disability, a chronic illness, or a death, won't be fixed here. The promise in Mosiah 24 is still real: strength to carry what isn't taken away, and the assurance that no blessing will finally be denied to the faithful.
- **Your pain counts too.** Caregivers often feel their own hurt is small next to the patient's. The Savior took on your sorrow as well as theirs. You can bring Him both.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Growth after trauma.** Psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun found that many people who go through serious hardship later report real growth: deeper relationships, a clearer sense of what matters, and stronger faith. They call it *posttraumatic growth*. It doesn't make the hardship good, and it usually comes alongside pain, not instead of it.
- **Healing takes time.** Whether the wound is physical, emotional, or relational, recovery is rarely a straight line. Expect good weeks and bad weeks.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Have you ever thought of the Atonement as being for your pain, and not only your sins? What changes if it is?
2. Read Mosiah 24:13–15. Has the Lord ever made a burden lighter for you without taking it away? How?
3. What burden are you trying to carry entirely by yourself right now?
4. What would it look like to "give" a specific worry to the Lord this week, practically speaking?

### Practice Card: Week 3
**Main practice: Hand it over**
Each morning, name one specific worry in prayer and ask the Savior to carry it with you that day. At night, notice whether it felt any lighter.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (wake up / start the car / sit down for breakfast), I will name one worry and hand it to the Lord.

**Also try:**
- **Study one verse deeply.** Spend ten minutes with Alma 7:11–13. Look up *succor* and *infirmities* in a dictionary.
- **Write a lament.** Like the Psalms, write God an honest letter about what hurts. End it with one thing you still trust about Him.

## Chapter 4: Draw Near unto Me
*Manual themes: God's guidance, Scripture study, The sacrament, The temple, Priesthood blessings, The Holy Ghost*

> "Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you; seek me diligently and ye shall find me; ask, and ye shall receive; knock, and it shall be opened unto you." (D&C 88:63)

### The Big Idea
A crisis can crowd out the very things that keep us spiritually steady. Scripture study slips, Church attendance gets hard, the temple feels far away. This principle is an invitation to keep coming close to God through the ordinary means He has given, even in small, imperfect ways. The promise runs both directions: as you draw near, He draws near.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **D&C 88:63.** The promise is mutual. We move toward Him and He moves toward us.
- **James 5:14–15.** "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up."
- **D&C 42:44.** "And if they die they shall die unto me, and if they live they shall live unto me." Priesthood blessings are given in faith and in submission to God's will.
- **Moroni 4:3.** In the sacrament we promise to remember Him, so that we "may always have his Spirit to be with" us.
- **John 14:18.** "I will not leave you comfortless: I will come to you."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "My message today is how to aid the healing process of the soul. It is a message to lead you and me to the Great Healer, the Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. It is a plan to read the scriptures, pray, ponder, repent if necessary, and be healed with the peace and joy of His Spirit." — Elder Robert D. Hales, ["Healing Soul and Body"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1998/10/healing-soul-and-body?lang=eng) (October 1998), after his third heart attack

> "I discovered that if I dwelt only upon my pain, it inhibited the healing process. I found that pondering was a very important element in the healing process for both soul and body." — Elder Robert D. Hales, ["Healing Soul and Body"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1998/10/healing-soul-and-body?lang=eng) (October 1998)

> "Although the Savior could heal all whom He would heal, this is not true of those who hold His priesthood authority. Mortal exercises of that authority are limited by the will of Him whose priesthood it is." — Elder Dallin H. Oaks, ["He Heals the Heavy Laden"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/10/he-heals-the-heavy-laden?lang=eng) (October 2006)

### In Real Life
- **When you can't get to church.** Hospital stays and home caregiving make attendance hard. Ask your bishop about having the sacrament brought to you. Watch or listen to conference talks while you drive or sit with your loved one.
- **Scripture study for tired people.** One verse read slowly is better than a chapter skimmed. Audio scriptures work well in waiting rooms.
- **The temple.** Even an occasional visit, or time on the temple grounds, can give perspective. Some families add a loved one's name to the temple prayer roll.
- **Priesthood blessings.** Ask for them, for your loved one and for yourself as the caregiver. A blessing may or may not bring healing. It reliably brings the Lord's attention and counsel.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Rituals steady people under stress.** Research on routines and rituals suggests that regular, meaningful practices help people feel more in control and less anxious when life is unpredictable. Daily prayer and weekly sacrament can act as anchors when everything else is changing.
- **Spiritual struggle is common.** Many faithful people go through stretches where prayer feels empty. Counselors who work with religious clients note that these seasons often pass, and that keeping small practices going through them helps.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Which of these, scripture, prayer, sacrament, temple, priesthood blessings, has slipped the most since this trial began? Which would be easiest to bring back in a small way?
2. When has the Holy Ghost given you guidance or peace in a hard situation before?
3. How do you feel about asking for a blessing when healing may not come? What would you hope to hear?
4. What would "drawing near" look like for you this week if it had to fit in ten minutes a day?

### Practice Card: Week 4
**Main practice: Ten minutes near**
Choose one way to draw near to God and do it for ten minutes a day: reading, listening to a talk, praying, or pondering in a quiet spot.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (sit in the waiting room / finish the dishes / put the kids down), I will spend ten minutes drawing near.

**Also try:**
- **Ask for a blessing.** For your loved one, for yourself, or both.
- **Make the sacrament personal.** This Sunday, during the sacrament, think of one specific thing you need help with and ask for it.

## Chapter 5: Take Heed unto Thyself
*Manual themes: We cannot control or cure them, Our own healing, Caring for ourselves, Learning about the affliction*

> "Take heed unto thyself, and unto the doctrine; continue in them: for in doing this thou shalt both save thyself, and them that hear thee." (1 Timothy 4:16)

### The Big Idea
You can love someone with everything you have and still not be able to cure them, fix them, or make their choices for them. Accepting that is one of the hardest parts of loving a person who suffers. The second half of this principle is just as important: you need care too. A caregiver who runs on empty eventually can't give at all. Taking care of yourself is not a break from loving your family. It's part of it.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **Mosiah 4:27.** "See that all these things are done in wisdom and order; for it is not requisite that a man should run faster than he has strength."
- **D&C 10:4.** "Do not run faster or labor more than you have strength and means provided."
- **Mark 6:31.** Jesus told His exhausted disciples, "Come ye yourselves apart into a desert place, and rest a while: for there were many coming and going, and they had no leisure so much as to eat."
- **1 Kings 19:4–8.** Elijah, worn out and wanting to die, lay down under a juniper tree. An angel didn't lecture him. The angel told him to eat and sleep, twice, "because the journey is too great for thee."
- **Luke 5:16.** "And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed." Even the Savior stepped away from the crowds.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "In all of this, we should remember King Benjamin's caution to 'see that all these things are done in wisdom and order; for it is not requisite that a man should run faster than he has strength.' I think of that inspired teaching whenever I feel inadequate, frustrated, or depressed." — Elder Dallin H. Oaks, ["The Great Plan of Happiness"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1993/10/the-great-plan-of-happiness?lang=eng) (October 1993)

> "Fatigue is the common enemy of us all—so slow down, rest up, replenish, and refill. Physicians promise us that if we do not take time to be well, we most assuredly will take time later on to be ill." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) (October 2013)

> "This same promise and effect applies to you mothers who are widowed or divorced, to you singles who are lonely, to you caregivers who are burdened, to you persons who are addicted, and to all of us whatever our burden." — Elder Dallin H. Oaks, ["He Heals the Heavy Laden"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/10/he-heals-the-heavy-laden?lang=eng) (October 2006)

### In Real Life
- **What you can't control:** the diagnosis, how fast treatment works, other people's choices, whether a loved one comes back to church.
- **What you can control:** how you show up, what you learn, who you ask for help, whether you sleep and eat, and how you talk to God.
- **Learn about the affliction.** The original guide had a section on understanding addiction. The same idea applies to any affliction. Learn what you can about the illness, disability, or condition from reliable sources: doctors, reputable organizations, other families who've been through it. Understanding why a person with depression can't "just get up," or why someone with dementia repeats themselves, makes patience much easier.
- **Respite is not abandonment.** Letting someone else take a shift so you can rest is wise. Accept the offer.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Caregiver burnout is real and common.** Long-term family caregivers have higher rates of depression, anxiety, and health problems than people who aren't caregiving. Warning signs include constant exhaustion, getting sick often, irritability, pulling away from friends, and feeling hopeless.
- **Compassion fatigue.** Charles Figley described how people who care for those who suffer can absorb that suffering and wear down over time. It isn't a lack of love. It's a sign you need rest and support.
- **The oxygen mask rule.** Airline crews tell parents to put on their own mask before helping their children. The logic applies to caregiving: you can't help anyone if you've passed out.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. What are you trying to control right now that isn't yours to control? What would it feel like to hand it to the Lord?
2. When did you last sleep well, eat a real meal, or spend an hour on something you enjoy?
3. What do you wish you understood better about your loved one's affliction? Where could you learn it?
4. Who has offered to help that you haven't taken up on it? What's stopping you?

### Practice Card: Week 5
**Main practice: One protected hour**
Put one hour on your calendar this week that is just for your own renewal: a walk, a nap, the temple, lunch with a friend. Arrange coverage if you need it, and keep the appointment like you would a doctor's visit.
> *My plan:* On ______ (day) at ______ (time), I will ______, and ______ will cover for me.

**Also try:**
- **Two lists.** Draw a line down a page. On one side, what you can control. On the other, what you can't. Pray over the second list and do one thing from the first.
- **Learn one thing.** Read one reliable article or chapter about your loved one's condition.

## Chapter 6: Thy Friends Do Stand by Thee
*Manual themes: Asking for support, The right kind of support, Family and friends, Ministering and leaders, Support groups, Professional help*

> "Thy friends do stand by thee, and they shall hail thee again with warm hearts and friendly hands." (D&C 121:9)

### The Big Idea
The Lord spoke these words to Joseph Smith while he was locked in Liberty Jail. Even there, He pointed Joseph to his friends. Affliction tends to isolate people. You may not want to burden anyone, or you may feel no one could understand. But the Lord works through people, and He has placed people around you to help carry the load. Part of faith is letting them.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **D&C 121:9.** In one of the darkest moments of Church history, the Lord reminded Joseph that he was not alone.
- **Exodus 17:12.** When Moses grew too tired to hold up his arms, "Aaron and Hur stayed up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side; and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun."
- **Ecclesiastes 4:9–10.** "Two are better than one. … For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth."
- **Mosiah 18:8–9.** At baptism we promised to "bear one another's burdens, that they may be light" and to "mourn with those that mourn." Letting others help you lets them keep that covenant.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "One of the great consolations of this Easter season is that because Jesus walked such a long, lonely path utterly alone, we do not have to do so. His solitary journey brought great company for our little version of that path—the merciful care of our Father in Heaven, the unfailing companionship of this Beloved Son, the consummate gift of the Holy Ghost, angels in heaven, family members on both sides of the veil, prophets and apostles, teachers, leaders, friends." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["None Were with Him"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2009/04/none-were-with-him?lang=eng) (April 2009)

> "When our minds are suffering, it is appropriate to seek help from God, from those around us, and from medical and mental health professionals." — Sister Reyna I. Aburto, ["Thru Cloud and Sunshine, Lord, Abide with Me!"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2019/10/31aburto?lang=eng) (October 2019)

> "In contrast, when we open up about our emotional challenges, admitting we are not perfect, we give others permission to share their struggles. Together we realize there is hope and we do not have to suffer alone." — Sister Reyna I. Aburto, ["Thru Cloud and Sunshine, Lord, Abide with Me!"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2019/10/31aburto?lang=eng) (October 2019)

### In Real Life
- **Family.** Some relatives will help well, and some won't know how. Give people specific jobs: "Could you sit with Dad on Tuesday afternoons?" works better than "Let me know if you need anything."
- **Friends.** You need at least one person you can be completely honest with, who won't try to fix you or lecture you.
- **Ministering brothers and sisters, the bishop, and Relief Society or elders quorum leaders.** They can coordinate meals, rides, and childcare, and the bishop can help with counseling and other resources. They can't help with what they don't know about.
- **Support groups.** There are groups for almost every situation: parents of children with disabilities, families dealing with mental illness (for example, NAMI's family programs), grief groups, cancer caregiver groups, and the Church's own family support groups through the Addiction Recovery Program.
- **Professional help.** Doctors, counselors, therapists, social workers, and hospice teams are part of how God answers prayers.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Support protects health.** Decades of research, beginning with work by Sheldon Cohen and Thomas Wills in the 1980s, show that strong social support buffers people against the effects of stress on both mind and body.
- **Not all support helps.** Some well-meaning comments hurt, like "Everything happens for a reason" or "At least…". It's fine to steer toward the people who listen well, and to gently set limits with those who don't.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Who are your "Aaron and Hur" right now? If you can't name anyone, who could become that?
2. What keeps you from asking for help? Pride, fear of being a burden, not knowing what to ask for?
3. What is one specific task someone else could take off your plate this month?
4. Has anyone ever said something "helpful" that hurt? How might you respond if it happens again?

### Practice Card: Week 6
**Main practice: Make one specific ask**
Write down one concrete thing you need help with. Ask one person this week, by name, with a day and time.
> *My plan:* By ______ (day), I will ask ______ to help with ______.

**Also try:**
- **Find your group.** Look up one support group for your situation, in person or online, and attend once.
- **Tell your leaders.** Let your bishop or ministering brothers and sisters know what's going on, even briefly.

## Chapter 7: In Everything Give Thanks
*Manual themes: Discouragement, fear, and anger, God's hand in our lives, Our own gifts, The good in our loved ones, Gratitude for the Savior*

> "Verily I say unto you my friends, fear not, let your hearts be comforted; yea, rejoice evermore, and in everything give thanks." (D&C 98:1)

### The Big Idea
The Lord says "in everything give thanks," not "for everything." No one is asked to be grateful that their child is sick or their spouse is gone. But even in the worst seasons, there are things to be grateful for, and noticing them changes how heavy the season feels. Gratitude doesn't deny pain. It keeps pain from becoming the only thing you can see.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **D&C 98:1.** The Saints received this revelation in August 1833, soon after mobs attacked them in Jackson County, Missouri.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:18.** "In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you."
- **Alma 34:38.** "Live in thanksgiving daily, for the many mercies and blessings which he doth bestow upon you."
- **D&C 78:19.** "He who receiveth all things with thankfulness shall be made glorious."
- **D&C 59:21.** God is displeased with those who "confess not his hand in all things." Looking for His hand is a commandment because it helps us.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "Could I suggest that we see gratitude as a disposition, a way of life that stands independent of our current situation? In other words, I'm suggesting that instead of being thankful for things, we focus on being thankful in our circumstances—whatever they may be." — President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, ["Grateful in Any Circumstances"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2014/04/grateful-in-any-circumstances?lang=eng) (April 2014)

> "My dear brothers and sisters, the joy we feel has little to do with the circumstances of our lives and everything to do with the focus of our lives." — President Russell M. Nelson, ["Joy and Spiritual Survival"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2016/10/joy-and-spiritual-survival?lang=eng) (October 2016)

> "Also let us remember that through any illness or difficult challenge, there is still much in life to be hopeful about and grateful for. We are infinitely more than our limitations or our afflictions!" — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) (October 2013)

### In Real Life
- **Discouragement, fear, and anger** are normal reactions to affliction. Tell God about them honestly. Gratitude works best alongside honesty, not as a way to cover feelings up.
- **Look for God's hand in small things:** a parking spot at the hospital, a text that came at the right moment, a good test result, a neighbor who mowed the lawn.
- **Notice your own gifts.** Many caregivers discover patience, strength, or skills they didn't know they had.
- **See the person, not just the affliction.** Your loved one is more than a diagnosis or a struggle. Make a point of noticing their humor, kindness, courage, and the things that are still them.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Gratitude practices have measurable effects.** In a well-known study (Emmons & McCullough, 2003), people who wrote down things they were grateful for each week felt more optimistic and reported fewer physical complaints than people who wrote about hassles.
- **Gratitude letters.** In Martin Seligman's research, writing and delivering a letter of thanks to someone produced one of the biggest short-term boosts in wellbeing of any exercise tested.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. What is one thing you're grateful for in this season that you would never have noticed before it started?
2. Where have you seen God's hand in your situation this month?
3. What do you love about your loved one that has nothing to do with their affliction?
4. Is there anger or fear you've been hiding behind a "grateful" face? What would it mean to bring it to God?

**Main practice: Find His hand**
Each night this week, answer one question in a sentence or two: "Where did I see God's hand today?" President Henry B. Eyring has described keeping a journal like this for years.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (get into bed / say my evening prayer), I will write where I saw God's hand.

**Also try:**
- **Write one thank-you note** to someone who has helped your family through this.
- **Tell your loved one** one specific thing you appreciate about them, unrelated to their illness or struggle.

## Chapter 8: Be Firm and Steadfast
*Manual themes: Healthy limits, Being open and honest, Protecting the rest of the family, Preserving relationships, We need not endure abuse*

> "Therefore, as many as have come to this, ye know of yourselves are firm and steadfast in the faith, and in the thing wherewith they have been made free." (Helaman 15:8)

### The Big Idea
In the original guide, this principle was about boundaries with a loved one in addiction. Boundaries matter in every kind of affliction. Caring for someone doesn't mean saying yes to everything, giving up every other responsibility, or letting the affliction run the whole family. Firm, loving limits protect your health, your marriage, your other children, and often the person you're caring for. And no affliction, whether illness, addiction, or mental illness, makes abuse acceptable.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **Helaman 15:8.** Being "firm and steadfast" is about knowing what you stand on.
- **Mosiah 4:27.** Doing things "in wisdom and order" includes deciding what you will and won't take on.
- **Matthew 22:39.** "Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself." Loving yourself is assumed in the commandment.
- **Luke 5:15–16.** Great crowds came to be healed, "And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed." The Savior had limits on His time and energy, and He kept them.
- **Ephesians 4:15.** "Speaking the truth in love." Limits are kinder when they're stated clearly and lovingly.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "As a result of their focusing too much time and energy on their Church service, eternal family relationships can deteriorate. Employment performance can suffer. This is not healthy, spiritually or otherwise." — Elder M. Russell Ballard, ["O Be Wise"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/10/o-be-wise?lang=eng) (October 2006)

> "We should never allow our service to replace the attention needed by other important priorities in our lives." — Elder M. Russell Ballard, ["O Be Wise"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/10/o-be-wise?lang=eng) (October 2006)

Elder Ballard was speaking about Church callings, but the same balance applies to caregiving.

### In Real Life
- **Limits on your time and energy.** You might decide you can do three nights a week at the hospital, not seven, and ask others to cover the rest.
- **Protecting other relationships.** Your marriage and your other children need you too. Keep a regular date, a one-on-one outing with each child, or family dinner when you can.
- **Limits with a loved one's choices.** If a family member's choices are harming the household, such as substance use in the home or pornography on shared devices, it's right to set clear expectations and consequences, as the original guide teaches.
- **Safety comes first.** Illness can sometimes cause aggression, as with some forms of dementia, brain injury, or psychosis. Addiction and untreated mental illness can lead to threats or violence. You are not required to stay in danger. Remove yourself and your children, call for help, and work with doctors and leaders on a safe plan. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is **1-800-799-7233**.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Boundaries reduce resentment.** Counselors who work with families find that caregivers who never say no often become resentful, and resentment eats away at love. Clear limits, kept consistently, usually make relationships warmer, not colder.
- **Helping vs. enabling.** In addiction, "enabling" means shielding someone from the consequences of their choices in a way that makes change less likely. The idea can apply elsewhere too, such as doing things for a loved one that they can, and need to, do for themselves as part of recovery.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Where are you saying yes when you need to say no?
2. Who else in your family is quietly paying a price for this affliction? What do they need from you?
3. Is there anything happening in your home that isn't safe? Who could you talk to about it this week?
4. What limit would you set if you weren't afraid of disappointing someone?

### Practice Card: Week 8
**Main practice: Name one limit**
Decide on one limit that would protect your health or your family, and say it out loud, kindly and clearly, to the person who needs to hear it.
> *My plan:* I will tell ______ that ______, by ______ (day).

**Also try:**
- **Protect one relationship.** Schedule one hour of undivided time with your spouse or one of your other children.
- **Make a safety plan** if anyone in your home has ever been in danger. Write down who to call and where to go.

- **Talks:** M. Russell Ballard, ["O Be Wise"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2006/10/o-be-wise?lang=eng) · Dallin H. Oaks, ["Focus and Priorities"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2001/04/focus-and-priorities?lang=eng)
- **Scriptures:** Mosiah 4:27 · Luke 5:15–16 · Helaman 15:5–8
- **Books:** *Boundaries*, Henry Cloud & John Townsend · *Beyond Addiction*, Jeffrey Foote, Carrie Wilkens & Nicole Kosanke (for families of people with addiction)

## Chapter 9: We Have Renounced Dishonesty
*Manual themes: Building trust, Honest talk in the family, Honesty with ourselves, Honesty with God, Regular communication*

> "[We] have renounced the hidden things of dishonesty, not walking in craftiness, nor handling the word of God deceitfully; but by manifestation of the truth commending ourselves to every man's conscience in the sight of God." (2 Corinthians 4:2)

### The Big Idea
Affliction invites hiding. Families keep a diagnosis quiet, avoid hard conversations, tell children "everything's fine," or pretend at church that nothing is wrong. Sometimes we hide things from ourselves, refusing to admit how bad it is or how tired we are. And sometimes we hide from God, praying polite prayers instead of honest ones. This principle invites truthfulness, offered with love, at every level. Trust grows where people can tell each other the truth.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **2 Corinthians 4:2.** Paul describes giving up "the hidden things."
- **Ephesians 4:15, 25.** "Speaking the truth in love" and "speak every man truth with his neighbour."
- **Psalm 62:8.** "Pour out your heart before him: God is a refuge for us."
- **Psalm 13:1.** "How long wilt thou forget me, O Lord? for ever?" The Psalms are full of honest complaint, and God kept them in scripture.
- **Matthew 26:38.** In Gethsemane the Savior told His friends plainly, "My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death: tarry ye here, and watch with me."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "Black clouds may also form in our lives, which can blind us to God's light and even cause us to question if that light exists for us anymore. Some of those clouds are of depression, anxiety, and other forms of mental and emotional affliction." — Sister Reyna I. Aburto, ["Thru Cloud and Sunshine, Lord, Abide with Me!"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2019/10/31aburto?lang=eng) (October 2019)

> "Does this mean we will always understand our challenges? Won't all of us, sometime, have reason to ask, 'O God, where art thou?' Yes!" — Elder Robert D. Hales, ["Waiting upon the Lord: Thy Will Be Done"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2011/10/waiting-upon-the-lord-thy-will-be-done?lang=eng) (October 2011)

> "Isn't it interesting that the one who is suffering often gains faith through suffering and accepts the Lord's will, 'thy will be done,' while family members and caregivers may have a difficult time accepting the tragic outcome and being able to strengthen their faith from the experience." — Elder Robert D. Hales, ["Finding Faith in the Lord Jesus Christ"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2004/10/finding-faith-in-the-lord-jesus-christ?lang=eng) (October 2004)

### In Real Life
- **Honesty with children.** Kids notice far more than adults think. Age-appropriate truth ("Grandma's brain is sick, and that's why she forgets things") is usually less frightening to them than silence.
- **Honesty with doctors and leaders.** Tell your loved one's care team what you actually see at home. Tell your bishop what you actually need.
- **Honesty with yourself.** Name how you're really doing. "I'm fine" said every day for a year usually isn't true.
- **Honesty with God.** He can handle your anger, your doubt, and your questions. Honest prayers are still prayers.
- **Regular check-ins.** In a family facing affliction, set a regular time to talk about how everyone is doing, not just the patient.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Secrets are heavy.** Research on secrecy by psychologist Michael Slepian suggests that the burden of a secret comes less from hiding it in conversation and more from how often our minds return to it. Sharing it with even one trusted person can lighten that load.
- **Writing helps.** James Pennebaker's studies found that people who wrote honestly about their deepest feelings on a hard experience for 15 to 20 minutes over a few days often showed better mood and health afterward.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. What are you not saying out loud, to someone else, to yourself, or to God?
2. What do your children or other family members know about what's happening? What might they be imagining?
3. When did you last pray a completely honest prayer? What happened?
4. Who is one person you could tell the whole truth to this week?

### Practice Card: Week 9
**Main practice: The honest prayer**
Once this week, find a private place and pray aloud for at least ten minutes. Tell Heavenly Father exactly how you feel about your situation, including the parts you think you shouldn't feel. Then sit quietly for a few minutes and listen.
> *My plan:* On ______ (day), when ______ (the house is quiet / I'm on my walk), I will pray an honest prayer.

**Also try:**
- **Family check-in.** Ask each person at dinner, "How are you really doing with all this?" and just listen.
- **Write it out.** Spend 15 minutes writing everything you feel about this trial. You don't have to keep it.

## Chapter 10: Lift Up the Hands Which Hang Down
*Manual themes: Supporting in righteousness, Encouragement, Continuing to minister, Serving others*

> "Wherefore, be faithful; stand in the office which I have appointed unto you; succor the weak, lift up the hands which hang down, and strengthen the feeble knees." (D&C 81:5)

### The Big Idea
This principle is about how to help the person who is suffering, and how serving others can heal us too. Good support is not the same as doing everything. It means encouraging, believing in the person, respecting their agency and dignity, and staying steady over time. It also means looking outward when you can. Many caregivers find that serving someone else, even in a small way, brings light back into their own life.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **D&C 81:5.** "Succor," "lift," and "strengthen" are all words about helping someone stand, not carrying them forever.
- **Galatians 6:2.** "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ."
- **Luke 10:33–34.** The Samaritan "had compassion on him, And went to him, and bound up his wounds." Then he arranged ongoing care at the inn.
- **Mosiah 2:17.** "When ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of your God."
- **John 13:14–15.** The Savior washed His disciples' feet and said, "I have given you an example."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "In addition, we must notice the tribulation of others and try to help. That will be especially hard when we are being sorely tested ourselves. But we will discover as we lift another's burden, even a little, that our backs are strengthened and we sense a light in the darkness." — President Henry B. Eyring, ["Tested, Proved, and Polished"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2020/10/51eyring?lang=eng) (October 2020)

> "While God is at work making those repairs, the rest of us can help by being merciful, nonjudgmental, and kind." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) (October 2013)

> "The challenges and difficulties were not immediately removed from the people. But Alma and his followers were strengthened, and their increased capacity made the burdens lighter." — Elder David A. Bednar, ["Bear Up Their Burdens with Ease"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2014/04/bear-up-their-burdens-with-ease?lang=eng) (April 2014)

### In Real Life
- **Encourage without pushing.** "I believe in you" and "I'm proud of how hard you're working" go further than advice.
- **Respect their agency and dignity.** Let your loved one make the choices they're able to make, even small ones, like what to wear or which treatment to try. Being asked matters.
- **Stay steady.** Many people help in the first few weeks of a crisis and then fade away. Being the person who is still there in month eight means a lot.
- **Serve someone else.** Caregivers sometimes find relief in serving outside the situation: visiting someone else in the hospital, helping another family who's just been diagnosed, or simply doing a ministering visit.
- **If you're on the outside of someone else's affliction,** notice the caregiver too. Bring the meal to the spouse, not just the patient. Ask how they are doing.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Helping helps the helper.** Studies on volunteering and helping behavior find links with lower stress and better mood for the person giving help. This fits what President Eyring describes: lifting someone else strengthens our own backs.
- **Autonomy supports recovery.** Across health psychology, people who feel they have some choice and control in their care tend to be more motivated and do better. Doing everything for someone can quietly take that away.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Is there anything you're doing for your loved one that they could do, or would want to do, themselves?
2. What kind of encouragement does your loved one respond to best?
3. Who else do you know who is carrying a heavy load right now? What small thing could you do for them?
4. Who has lifted your hands during this trial? Have you told them what it meant?

### Practice Card: Week 10
**Main practice: Lift someone else**
Once this week, do one small act of service for someone outside your own situation: a note, a meal, a visit, a phone call.
> *My plan:* This week I will ______ for ______, on ______ (day).

**Also try:**
- **One sentence of encouragement.** Each day, tell your loved one one specific thing you admire about how they're handling this.
- **Ask, don't assume.** Ask your loved one, "What would actually help you most this week?"

## Chapter 11: Bear All These Things with Patience
*Manual themes: Healing is a process, Setbacks and hard days, When healing doesn't come here, Helping through setbacks*

> "For I know that thou wast in bonds; yea … thou didst bear all these things with patience because the Lord was with thee; and now thou knowest that the Lord did deliver thee." (Alma 38:4)

### The Big Idea
Most afflictions don't follow a straight line. Treatments work and then stop working. A loved one in recovery relapses. Grief that seemed to be easing comes back on an anniversary. Some afflictions will not be healed in this life at all. Patience, in the gospel sense, isn't passive. It is choosing to keep going, keep loving, and keep trusting God over a long road, including the setbacks. Alma's son Shiblon was delivered, but only after he had borne his bonds "with patience because the Lord was with" him.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **Alma 38:4.** The Lord was with Shiblon during the bonds, not only at the deliverance.
- **D&C 24:8.** "Be patient in afflictions, for thou shalt have many; but endure them, for, lo, I am with thee, even unto the end of thy days."
- **2 Corinthians 12:7–9.** Paul prayed three times for his "thorn in the flesh" to be removed. The Lord's answer was, "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness."
- **D&C 121:7–8.** "Thine adversity and thine afflictions shall be but a small moment; And then, if thou endure it well, God shall exalt thee on high."
- **Lamentations 3:22–23.** "His compassions fail not. They are new every morning." Each hard day can start over.

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "There is an important concept here: patience is not passive resignation, nor is it failing to act because of our fears. Patience means active waiting and enduring. It means staying with something and doing all that we can—working, hoping, and exercising faith; bearing hardship with fortitude, even when the desires of our hearts are delayed. Patience is not simply enduring; it is enduring well!" — President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, ["Continue in Patience"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2010/04/continue-in-patience?lang=eng) (April 2010)

> "Recognize that some challenges in life will not be resolved here on earth. Paul pled thrice that 'a thorn in the flesh' be removed. The Lord simply answered, 'My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.'" — Elder Richard G. Scott, ["To Be Healed"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1994/04/to-be-healed?lang=eng) (April 1994)

> "For every infirm man healed instantly as he waits to enter the Pool of Bethesda, someone else will spend 40 years in the desert waiting to enter the promised land." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Waiting on the Lord"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2020/10/57holland?lang=eng) (October 2020)

> "Some blessings come soon, some come late, and some don't come until heaven; but for those who embrace the gospel of Jesus Christ, they come." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["An High Priest of Good Things to Come"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/1999/10/an-high-priest-of-good-things-to-come?lang=eng) (October 1999)

### In Real Life
- **Relapse in addiction or mental illness** is common and doesn't erase progress. Respond with calm, clear support and the limits you've already set, not panic or shame.
- **Flare-ups in chronic illness** can feel like going back to the beginning. They usually aren't. Track the longer trend, not just this week.
- **Grief comes in waves.** Birthdays, holidays, and anniversaries often bring it back. Plan for those days and let people know.
- **When the answer is "not in this life."** Some families have to accept that a disability will stay, a disease is terminal, or a loved one may never return. That acceptance isn't giving up on God. Paul kept serving with his thorn. You can keep living, loving, and hoping with yours.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Recovery isn't linear.** In addiction treatment, relapse is widely understood as a common part of recovery rather than proof of failure. Similar patterns show up in depression and many chronic illnesses.
- **Grief oscillates.** Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut's "dual process model" describes healthy grieving as moving back and forth between facing the loss and taking breaks from it to handle daily life. Having good days doesn't mean you didn't love the person.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. What setback has been hardest for you so far? How did you respond, and what would you do the same or differently?
2. Where is the "longer trend" better than it feels this week?
3. Is there an affliction in your family you may need to accept won't end in this life? What would that acceptance look like with faith?
4. What does "enduring well" mean to you, in practical terms, this month?

### Practice Card: Week 11
**Main practice: A setback plan**
Write down what you'll do the next time there's a bad day, a relapse, or a flare-up: who you'll call, what you'll say to yourself, which scripture you'll read, and one kind thing you'll do for yourself.
> *My plan:* When the next hard day comes, I will ______, call ______, and read ______.

**Also try:**
- **Mark the hard dates.** Put upcoming anniversaries or appointments on the calendar with a reminder to plan support for that day.
- **Look back.** Write down three ways things are better than they were six months ago, even small ones.

## Chapter 12: My Peace I Give unto You
*Manual themes: Finding peace, Forgiveness, God's help to forgive, Hope beyond this life*

> "Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid." (John 14:27)

### The Big Idea
The Savior offers a peace that doesn't depend on circumstances. He said it is "not as the world giveth." The world's peace comes when problems are solved. His comes even while they aren't. Part of receiving it is letting go of what poisons the heart: resentment toward a loved one whose choices hurt the family, anger at a doctor or a relative, bitterness at how unfair it all is. Forgiveness doesn't excuse wrong or require you to stay in harm's way. It frees you. And the deepest peace comes from knowing how the story ends: every wrong made right, every loss restored, and every tear wiped away.

### What the Scriptures Teach
- **John 14:27.** He spoke these words on the night before His death, knowing what was coming.
- **Philippians 4:7.** "The peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus."
- **D&C 64:10.** "I, the Lord, will forgive whom I will forgive, but of you it is required to forgive all men."
- **John 16:33.** "In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world."
- **Revelation 21:4.** "God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain."

### From Prophets and Apostles
> "Through His infinite Atonement, you can forgive those who have hurt you and who may never accept responsibility for their cruelty to you." — President Russell M. Nelson, as quoted by Sister Kristin M. Yee, ["Beauty for Ashes: The Healing Path of Forgiveness"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2022/10/23yee?lang=eng) (October 2022)

> "On the path of forgiveness, Jesus Christ's atoning power can flow into our lives and begin to heal the deep crevasses of the heart and soul." — Sister Kristin M. Yee, ["Beauty for Ashes: The Healing Path of Forgiveness"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2022/10/23yee?lang=eng) (October 2022)

> "The truth is, you will never completely get over it until you are together once again with your departed loved ones. I will never have a fulness of joy until we are reunited in the morning of the First Resurrection." — Elder Shayne M. Bowen, ["'Because I Live, Ye Shall Live Also'"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2012/10/because-i-live-ye-shall-live-also?lang=eng) (October 2012)

> "Know that one day the dawn will break brightly and all shadows of mortality will flee." — Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, ["Like a Broken Vessel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2013/10/like-a-broken-vessel?lang=eng) (October 2013)

### In Real Life
- **Forgiving a loved one.** If the affliction involves someone's choices, you may carry real hurt. Forgiveness can begin before trust is rebuilt, and it doesn't erase the limits you've set.
- **Forgiving others.** A careless comment at church, a relative who disappeared when you needed them, a medical mistake. Holding onto these keeps the wound open.
- **Forgiving yourself.** For the times you lost patience, missed something, or said the wrong thing. The Savior's Atonement covers your mistakes as a caregiver too.
- **Anger at God.** Many people feel it, and it's better to bring it to Him than to hide it. Peace often comes after that kind of honest wrestling.
- **Peace in grief.** The plan of salvation doesn't remove the ache of losing someone, but it changes what the ache means. Separation is temporary.

### Also Worth Knowing
- **Forgiveness and health.** Studies by Everett Worthington, Robert Enright, and others link forgiveness with lower stress, less anxiety and depression, and better physical health. Researchers are careful to separate forgiveness from reconciliation: you can forgive someone without restoring a relationship that isn't safe.
- **"Continuing bonds."** Grief researchers (Klass, Silverman, and Nickman, 1996) found that many bereaved people keep a meaningful connection with the person who died, and that this is healthy, not a failure to move on. Latter-day Saint belief in eternal families gives that connection deep roots.

### Questions Worth Sitting With
1. Who do you need to forgive in this trial, including possibly yourself?
2. What would change in your heart if you let go of one specific resentment this week?
3. When have you felt the Savior's peace even though nothing around you had changed?
4. How does your belief in the Resurrection and eternal families shape how you face this affliction?

### Practice Card: Week 12
**Main practice: Begin forgiving**
Choose one person you need to forgive. Each day this week, pray for them by name and ask the Savior to help you let go of the hurt. You don't need to feel it yet. Start with the prayer.
> *My plan:* When I ______ (kneel for evening prayer / finish scripture study), I will pray for ______ by name.

**Also try:**
- **Look ahead.** Read Alma 40:23 and Revelation 21:1–4 and write down what you most look forward to being made right.
- **Look back.** Reread your comfort list from Week 1 and your "God's hand" notes from Week 7. Notice how much you've been carried.

## Appendix A: Finding support

### People and places to turn to
- **Your bishop or branch president** can provide counsel, help with practical needs, and refer you to Family Services for counseling.
- **Relief Society and elders quorum presidencies and your ministering brothers and sisters** can organize meals, rides, childcare, and visits.
- **Family Services** offers counseling and other help through referral from your bishop.
- **The Church's Addiction Recovery Program** runs support groups for family members as well as for people in recovery: [addictionrecovery.churchofjesuschrist.org](https://addictionrecovery.churchofjesuschrist.org)
- **Life Help** on the Church's website has resources on topics like mental health, disability, grief, abuse, and suicide: [churchofjesuschrist.org/study/life-help](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/life-help?lang=eng)
- **Community and medical resources:** hospital social workers, hospice and palliative care teams, disease-specific organizations, NAMI (mental illness), and local grief and caregiver groups.

### Choosing a support person
The original guide encourages each person to have at least one support person: someone you can call when things are hard. A good support person:

- Keeps what you tell them confidential
- Listens more than they advise
- Points you toward the Savior without preaching at you
- Is steady and available, and honest with you when you need it

It can be a spouse, a friend, a family member, a ministering brother or sister, or someone who has been through a similar trial.

### Being a good support person for someone else
- **Show up and keep showing up.** Consistency matters more than eloquence.
- **Ask, then listen.** "How are you doing with all of this?" followed by silence is often the most helpful thing you can offer.
- **Offer specific help.** "I'm bringing dinner Thursday" is easier to accept than "Let me know if you need anything."
- **Don't explain their trial to them.** Avoid "Everything happens for a reason" and "At least…". Mourn with them first.
- **Remember the caregiver.** The person caring for the patient is often running on empty.

## Appendix B: For group leaders

### A simple meeting outline (about 50 minutes)
1. **Welcome and prayer** (3 min)
2. **Report** (10 min): How did last week's Practice Card go? Celebrate small wins. Nobody has to share.
3. **Read the Big Idea and the scriptures together** (10 min)
4. **Discussion** (20 min): Pick two or three of the Questions Worth Sitting With. Use the quotes if things stall.
5. **This week's Practice Card** (5 min): Each person fills in their own plan.
6. **Closing prayer** (2 min)

### Keeping the group safe
- **What's said in the group stays in the group.**
- **Share your own experience,** not advice for others, unless someone asks.
- **No comparing trials.** Everyone's affliction is hard for them.
- **Watch for warning signs.** If anyone mentions thoughts of suicide, abuse, or danger, follow up privately, share the crisis numbers, and involve the bishop.
